Hey there, Yang Hong here. Today is February 24, 2026 — hoping you stay in good.
The Supreme Court just handed American businesses a $175 billion tax refund. That's the headline. Here's the problem — the administration that collected the money is now fighting in court to keep it. You paid the tax. They spent the tax. And now they're arguing about whether giving it back would be too disruptive. For the Treasury.

first of all, Forget the politics. Forget Trump versus the courts. What we're actually watching is a financial plumbing problem — and the pipes were never designed to run in reverse.
When Trump imposed his tariffs over the past year, money moved in one direction. Importers paid duties to Customs. Customs dropped that money into the Treasury's General Fund. Done. Spent. Absorbed into the federal budget.
Now the Supreme Court says those tariffs were illegal. Great. Refund time, right?
Not quite. 

The tariffs were charged to something called the Importer of Record — the legal entity that signs the customs paperwork. That's your Walmart. Your FedEx. Your mid-size manufacturer in Ohio. Those companies paid the duties on paper.
But who actually funded it? Think about your last Amazon order. Or your grocery run. The tariff cost was baked into the price before it ever hit the shelf. Studies suggest that 96 cents of every tariff dollar was paid by American consumers — not foreign exporters, not corporations absorbing the hit. You. At the checkout counter.
Now, to be fair — that's an average. Some companies did absorb costs. Some passed less than others. The picture varies by industry and product. But the direction is clear. Consumers funded the bulk of this.
And yet — legally — consumers don't exist in this refund process. You're not the Importer of Record. You have no standing to file in the Court of International Trade. You get nothing. The refund flows to the corporate layer above you.

So what happens to the $175 billion?
Here's where it gets messy. FedEx just became the first company to sue for a full refund — filed Monday in the Court of International Trade, which the Supreme Court confirmed has exclusive jurisdiction over these cases. Expect hundreds more suits to follow.
But here's the math problem. That $175 billion doesn't sit in a vault. It's been spent. It's in the budget. Treasury Secretary Bessent told CNN on Sunday that a refund decision could be "weeks or months away." And behind the scenes, the DOJ — Trump's own DOJ — has signaled it will fight individual refund claims in court.
Think about what that means structurally. The administration's own lawyers told the Supreme Court the refund process would be "a legal breeze." Now they're saying it'll be complicated. That's not a legal position. That's a delay strategy.
And the delay hits hardest where you'd expect — the little guys. A small importer who paid $40,000 in illegal tariffs now has to hire a trade lawyer, file in a federal court, and wait potentially 12 to 18 months. The legal bill might exceed the refund. So they walk away. The $175 billion slowly concentrates toward the companies big enough to litigate.
Not everyone gets hit this badly. Large importers with existing legal teams are in a far stronger position. But the structure of the refund process rewards scale — and punishes everyone else.

Meanwhile, Trump didn't pause. He dusted off Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — a law written for gold standard crises that haven't existed since 1971 — and slapped a 10% universal tariff back on by Tuesday midnight. He wanted 15%. For now it's 10%. But the White House is already working to push it higher.
Section 122 has a hard ceiling of 150 days and 15%. After that, he needs Congressional approval. And with midterm elections coming, Republican members of Congress are watching their approval numbers crater on the economy. The math on getting an extension through Congress is... not good.
So the risk scenario here isn't a crash. It's a grind. Businesses can't plan around a tariff that might be 10%, might be 15%, might expire in July, might get extended. That uncertainty is itself a tax on investment. And the refunds that could inject real cash back into the supply chain are being slow-walked through a legal process designed for a fraction of this volume.

The Supreme Court ruling looked like a $175 billion win. And for the biggest importers with the best lawyers, it probably is. But for the American consumer who actually funded this? There's no mechanism for your money to come back. And for the small businesses caught in the middle? The refund process may cost more than it's worth.
